Around 750 BC, Phrygia had been established, with its two centers in Gordium and modern-day Kayseri.[68] Phrygians spoke an Indo-European language, which was closer to Greek than Anatolian languages.[59] Phrygians shared Anatolia with Neo-Hittites and Urartu. Luwian-speakers were probably the majority in various Anatolian Neo-Hittite states.[69] Urartians spoke a non-Indo-European language and their capital was around Lake Van.[70][68] Urartu and Phrygia fell in seventh century BC.[68][71] They were replaced by Carians, Lycians and Lydians.[71] These three cultures "can be considered a reassertion of the ancient, indigenous culture of the Hattian cities of Anatolia".[71]

In the late Bronze Age, there were three[72] or four[73] Greek-speaking settlements in Anatolia, including Miletus.[72][73] Around 1000 BC, Greeks started migrating to the west coast of Anatolia.[68] These eastern Greek settlements played a vital role in shaping the Archaic Greek civilization;[68][74] important cities included Miletus, Ephesus, Halicarnassus, Smyrna (now İzmir) and Byzantium (now Istanbul).[75] These settlements were grouped as Aeolis, Ionia, and Doris, after the specific Greek groups that settled them.[76][77] Further Greek colonization in Anatolia was led by Miletus and Megara in 750–480 BC.[78]
